\begin{abstract}
Agents that compile solved tasks into reusable programs must decide when to replay one. We
compare two ways of deciding at matched coverage: executable preconditions, shell probes that
must hold on the live state, and text similarity between the request and each program's
description. On states built by our own injectors for five git maintenance faults, precondition
dispatch mis-fired on 0 of 563 fires across three independently built libraries, while three
text scorers mis-fired on 71--90\% of theirs. But a decision tree that only reads the state
matched it, and the request carried no information about the state by construction.
A pre-registered test on 274 real lock-file merges from 11 repositories, labelled by what maintainers
committed, \emph{falsified} the claim that precondition dispatch rarely mis-fires: it was wrong
on 62 of 150, 10 of 122 and 132 of 244 fires. Its margin came from merges without a conflict; on
conflicts, the library's line-level resolutions could express 1 of 161 maintainer resolutions.
Two further registered studies, on 47 fresh repositories, read lock files as
package sets. An action computed from a merge's three sides reproduces the maintainer on 0.69
and 0.76 of a repository's conflicts, against 0.07 for line unions, and the ecosystem's resolver
predicts which merge maintainers keep (91 of 91 discordant files). Exploratory analysis shows
why: half of lock-file conflicts involve no package both sides changed differently. Firing the
merge only when none diverges, itself a precondition, roughly halves its mismatch; a fifth
registered study, on 20 more repositories, confirms it stays above 0.10 (0.35 [0.18, 0.53]). We mutation-test the graders behind every figure, run a Monte Carlo power
analysis that amended a registered study before its data existed, and compare the design with a
deployed executability gate and a skill-retrieval risk benchmark.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

LLM agents increasingly reuse their own past work. A task solved once is compiled into a program
that can be replayed later without a model call
\cite{fikes1972macrops,cai2024latm,wang2023voyager,chen2026skilldroid,li2026preact,jaber2026auto}.
Replay is cheap, but only useful if the agent replays the right program. Most systems decide by
\emph{similarity}: they embed the request and retrieve the closest stored skill
\cite{wang2023voyager,chen2026skilldroid}. An older idea attaches \emph{executable
preconditions} to each program and fires it only when they hold on the live state, as MACROPS
did for generalized robot plans \cite{fikes1972macrops}.

The mechanism is not new, and neither is the argument that the most similar case is not always
the most reusable one \cite{smyth1998adaptation}. What we could not find is a head-to-head
measurement:

\begin{quote}
\emph{At matched dispatch coverage, does executable-precondition dispatch mis-fire less often
than text-similarity dispatch, where a mis-fire is a program that fires, claims it succeeded, and
did not?}
\end{quote}

Table~\ref{tab:settings} gives the answer this report reaches, for the same dispatchers in three
settings: states our injectors built, states built on seeds, paths and names no build saw, and
real merges graded by their maintainers.

Two recent works bracket the question. A deterministic executability gate deployed in a
customer-care agent removes candidate skills whose exit conditions hold, after semantic recall
\cite{wix2026gating}. A retrieval benchmark measures how often a ``risky sibling'' skill, one
from the right capability family that violates the query's execution contract, is exposed in the
top results \cite{ding2026rightfamily}. Neither compares the two decision rules at matched
coverage, and neither grades the executed choice against an outside label. Section~\ref{sec:comparison}
compares them with this work in detail.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A measurement design} for dispatch-level comparison (Section~\ref{sec:design}):
  mismatch at matched coverage with a fixed matching rule, a baseline floor for the text
  scorer, disjoint seed blocks, two-sided admission, and a proof that the uninformed request
  channel's text-control AUC of 0.5 is an identity, not a measurement.
  \item \textbf{Where the result holds and where it does not} (Sections~\ref{sec:synthetic}
  and~\ref{sec:real}): zero mis-fires on our own states, matched by a state reader; a
  falsified claim on real merges, under a registration fixed before the data was mined; and
  per-fault and per-repository breakdowns that locate each difference.
  \item \textbf{A validity analysis of the graders} (Section~\ref{sec:graders}): 171 mutants,
  each of the eight survivors that could change a verdict analysed, five closed by new tests and
  three argued equivalent.
  \item \textbf{A power analysis of the registered follow-up study, run through its own decision
  code} (Section~\ref{sec:power}), which confirms its primary test and led to an amendment of
  its non-inferiority guard before any of its data exists.
  \item \textbf{An external oracle for real lock-file conflicts} (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}): three
  registered studies on 67 repositories, an anatomy of what conflicts at the level of packages,
  and a precondition a dispatcher could deploy, tested after it was found.
  \item \textbf{A comparison with executability gating and retrieval-risk measurement}
  (Section~\ref{sec:comparison}), including our own measurement of the retrieve-then-gate
  cascade those systems use, and a taxonomy of where wrong fires come from
  (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Compile once, replay cheaply.}
LATM has a strong model build reusable tools that a cheaper model then calls \cite{cai2024latm}.
Voyager stores executable skills and retrieves them by embedding similarity
\cite{wang2023voyager}. SkillDroid compiles mobile GUI trajectories into parameterized templates,
replays them with no LLM call and routes new instructions through a matching cascade
\cite{chen2026skilldroid}. PreAct compiles a computer-use trace into a state-machine program that
checks the screen before each action \cite{li2026preact}. Auto compiles deterministic spans of
agent behaviour into sandboxed programs behind calibrated guards, and reports that a loose guard
silently mislabels many compiled answers \cite{jaber2026auto}. These systems establish that
replay can be cheap. None compares how the replay decision is made.

\paragraph{Precondition-based dispatch.}
MACROPS dispatches a cached plan by testing precondition kernels against the current state and
replans when they fail \cite{fikes1972macrops}. Soar's chunking re-fires compiled rules whose
conditions match \cite{laird1986chunking}. An option's initiation set plays a similar role in
reinforcement learning \cite{sutton1999options}. Smyth and Keane argue that similarity is a poor
proxy for adaptability in case-based reasoning \cite{smyth1998adaptation}.

\paragraph{Pre-registration and grader validity.}
We register hypotheses, cases and decision rules before data exists, binding the code to the
registration by a hash, in the spirit of \cite{nosek2018preregistration}. We test the graders
with mutation testing \cite{jia2011mutation} and property-based testing
\cite{maciver2019hypothesis}.

\section{Measurement design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Task family, states and seed blocks}

Tasks are maintenance faults injected into disposable git sandboxes. A fault is admitted to the
family only if it recurs, can be checked without a model, and has more than one correct
resolution depending on the state. Five are git faults: \code{dirty\_tree}, \code{diverged},
\code{submodule\_moved}, \code{branch\_renamed} and \code{lockfile\_conflict}. A sixth,
\code{config\_renamed}, changes files rather than history. Each fault has an \emph{intent} with
named resolutions, for example \code{take\_upstream} and \code{keep\_local} for a lock-file
conflict. A rule over the observed state, a \code{StateFingerprint} of 27 git-derived
fields, gives each state's set of acceptable resolutions.

An injector draws each state's shape along declared axes from its seed: which files, how many
commits on each side, where the conflict falls. So a new seed is a new environment rather than
the same shape with new hashes. Seeds come in disjoint blocks (Table~\ref{tab:seeds}).

\subsection{Programs and two-sided admission}

An LLM agent solves an episode in a sandbox. The solved trace is compiled into a program: its
parameters, its \emph{preconditions} (shell probes, all of which must hold for it to fire), a
body, and postconditions. A program is admitted only through a two-sided gate:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Contract checks, before any sandbox.} Its \code{variant} must be one of the
  intent's declared resolutions, and every parameter the body uses must appear in some
  precondition, so a program declares what it needs.
  \item \textbf{Positive side.} On a freshly faulted sandbox its preconditions must hold, and
  after the body runs its postconditions must hold.
  \item \textbf{Negative side.} Its preconditions must refuse three classes of state: a
  fault-free sandbox; every unrelated fault's states, one per distinct injected state; and the
  same intent's other states, where a sibling resolution is the right one. A probe the guard
  refuses, or one that changes the sandbox, refuses the program rather than counting as a
  ``no''.
  \item \textbf{Breadth cap.} Over a fixed universe of sampled states, its preconditions may
  hold on at most half.
\end{enumerate}

Each library is built once from a fixed plan of episodes, one per declared resolution, and then
frozen. Dispatch never writes to it.

\subsection{Dispatchers}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Precondition dispatch (arm 3)} evaluates every admitted program's probes on the
  state and fires the first whose preconditions all hold. It makes no model call and has no
  score, so it is a single operating point.
  \item \textbf{Text similarity (arm 2)} scores each program's description against the request.
  There are three scorers: a lexical scorer; a pinned bi-encoder, \code{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}
  \cite{reimers2019sbert}; and a pinned cross-encoder, \code{ms-marco-MiniLM-L-12-v2}, the
  strongest of 13 local models screened on the floor below. Arm 2 fires its top program when
  its score clears a threshold, and the threshold is swept.
  \item \textbf{The state reader} is one decision tree per intent over generic features of the
  fingerprint: every field as a scalar, the size of every pair of list fields' overlap, and
  whether every pair of string fields is equal. No feature names a rule. It is fitted on the
  build's 56 admit-seed states, labelled by the intents' rules.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The index: mismatch at matched coverage}

\begin{definition}
For a dispatcher $D$ on pairs $P$, let $F(D)\subseteq P$ be the pairs it fires on and $W(D)
\subseteq F(D)$ those where its fire is not in the pair's acceptable set, including any fire on a
pair whose acceptable set is empty. \emph{Coverage} is $c(D)=|F(D)|/|P|$, and \emph{mismatch} is
$m(D)=|W(D)|/|F(D)|$, undefined when $F(D)=\emptyset$.
\end{definition}

Mismatch is scored apart from episode success. A wrong program can fail its postconditions, fall
back to the agent, and still end the episode successfully, which is exactly why success cannot
detect a wrong fire.

\paragraph{The matching rule.}
Arm 3 is one point $(c_3, m_3)$. Arm 2 is swept over every distinct score it produced, so its
curve holds every achievable operating point and no interpolated one. The \emph{matched} point is
arm 2's point nearest $c_3$, distances compared as exact fractions, with a tie going to the
\emph{lower} coverage. Lower coverage usually has lower arm-2 mismatch, so the tie-break cannot be
what makes arm 3 look better. The coverage gap is reported beside every difference.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.}
Each rate carries a 95\% Wilson interval \cite{wilson1927}. For $k$ wrong fires out of $n$, with
$\hat p=k/n$ and $z=z_{0.975}$, the interval is
\[
  \frac{\hat p+\frac{z^2}{2n}\;\pm\;z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}+\frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1+\frac{z^2}{n}}.
\]
It is the set of rates that a score test at the 5\% level would not reject. Unlike the normal
interval $\hat p\pm z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$, it stays inside $[0,1]$ and does not shrink to a
point at $\hat p=0$. For precondition dispatch's $0/187$, its upper end is
$\frac{z^2/n}{1+z^2/n}=0.020$.

\paragraph{The smallest detectable difference.}
Before comparing two arms, the design states the smallest difference in mismatch that their fire
counts could reliably detect. We call it $\delta^\ast$ and derive it here, step by step. The code
computes it in \code{bench.coverage.detectable\_difference}, and a test pins its value at 187
fires per arm.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The model.} Arm $a\in\{2,3\}$ fires $n_a$ times. Each fire is wrong
  independently with probability $m_a$. So its wrong count is $W_a\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n_a,m_a)$,
  and its estimated mismatch is $\hat m_a=W_a/n_a$, with mean $m_a$ and variance
  $m_a(1-m_a)/n_a$. The two arms are treated as independent samples. The estimated difference
  $\hat\delta=\hat m_2-\hat m_3$ then has mean $\delta=m_2-m_3$ and variance
  \[
    \sigma^2=\frac{m_2(1-m_2)}{n_2}+\frac{m_3(1-m_3)}{n_3}.
  \]
  \item \textbf{The normal approximation.} For fire counts in the hundreds, the central limit
  theorem gives $\hat\delta\approx\mathcal N(\delta,\sigma^2)$.
  \item \textbf{The test.} The null hypothesis is $\delta=0$. Under it both arms share one rate
  $\bar m$, so the variance in step 1 becomes $\sigma_0^2=\bar m(1-\bar m)(1/n_2+1/n_3)$. A
  two-sided test at level $\alpha$ rejects when $|\hat\delta|>z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma_0$, where
  $z_q$ is the standard normal's $q$-quantile, $\Phi(z_q)=q$.
  \item \textbf{The power.} Suppose the true difference is $\delta>0$. The probability of
  rejecting is
  \[
    \Pr\!\left[\hat\delta>z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma_0\right]+\Pr\!\left[\hat\delta<-z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma_0\right]
    =\Phi\!\left(\frac{\delta-z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma_0}{\sigma}\right)
    +\Phi\!\left(\frac{-\delta-z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma_0}{\sigma}\right).
  \]
  The second term is rejection in the wrong direction. It is below $\alpha/2$ for any
  $\delta>0$, and at the answer below it is about $10^{-6}$, so we drop it.
  \item \textbf{One more approximation.} We evaluate the variance under the alternative at the
  same base rate, $\sigma\approx\sigma_0$. The power is then
  $\Phi\!\left(\delta/\sigma_0-z_{1-\alpha/2}\right)$.
  \item \textbf{Solving for $\delta$.} Requiring power $1-\beta$ gives
  $\Phi(\delta/\sigma_0-z_{1-\alpha/2})=1-\beta$. Since $\Phi^{-1}(1-\beta)=z_{1-\beta}$, this
  means $\delta/\sigma_0-z_{1-\alpha/2}=z_{1-\beta}$, and so
  \[
    \delta^\ast=\left(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta}\right)\sigma_0
    =\left(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta}\right)\sqrt{\bar m(1-\bar m)\left(\tfrac{1}{n_2}+\tfrac{1}{n_3}\right)}.
  \]
  With the conventional $\alpha=0.05$ and power $1-\beta=0.8$, the factor is
  $z_{0.975}+z_{0.8}$.
  \item \textbf{The base rate.} $\bar m(1-\bar m)$ is largest at $\bar m=\tfrac12$, where it is
  $\tfrac14$. Using $\bar m=0.5$ therefore gives the largest $\delta^\ast$ for given fire counts,
  a statement of sensitivity that a low observed rate cannot flatter. On replicate 1 the
  observed pooled rate is $148/374=0.396$ (lexical similarity's 148 wrong of 187 fires and
  precondition dispatch's 0 of 187), which would give $0.142$.
  \item \textbf{The numbers.} $z_{0.975}=1.95996$ and $z_{0.8}=0.84162$, so the factor is
  $2.80159$. At $n_2=n_3=187$ and $\bar m=0.5$,
  $\sigma_0=\sqrt{0.25\times 2/187}=0.05171$, and
  \[
    \delta^\ast=2.80159\times0.05171=0.145.
  \]
\end{enumerate}

Three of these steps approximate, and we state which way each one errs.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Independence (step 1).} Each pair is its own sandbox, but both arms are scored on
  the same 400 pairs, and they fire on overlapping subsets of them. The formula treats the two
  fire sets as independent samples.
  \item \textbf{Normality (step 2).} The approximation is weakest near 0 and 1, which is where
  precondition dispatch sits. This is why every reported difference also carries Wilson or
  bootstrap intervals, and $\delta^\ast$ is used only as a statement made at the design stage.
  \item \textbf{The shared variance (step 5).} It overstates $\sigma$ when the rates are far
  apart. With $m_3=0$ and $m_2=0.145$, the true $\sigma$ is
  $\sqrt{0.145\times0.855/187}=0.026$, half of $\sigma_0$. So the real power at $\delta^\ast$
  exceeds 0.8, and $\delta^\ast$ is conservative.
\end{itemize}
In Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} the observed gaps are 0.71 to 0.90, which is five to six times
$\delta^\ast$. Precondition dispatch's mismatch there is 0, so each gap is the text scorer's own
mismatch: from 133 of 187 to 169 of 187 fires.

\paragraph{Vacuity.}
Half of each block's 400 pairs are fault-free negatives, where the right decision is not to fire,
so a dispatcher that never mis-fires covers at most 0.5. While arm 3 decides every pair
correctly, the comparison says nothing about arm 3's own error rate, and the report says so.

\subsection{The baseline floor}

A weak text scorer would make any comparison trivially won. Arm 2 therefore counts as a baseline
only if the 95\% Wilson lower bound of its strict top-1 accuracy, on the tune block's informed
requests against the evaluated library, exceeds chance, $1/3$ for three candidates. Below the
floor the comparison is still reported, but ``precondition dispatch beats text similarity'' may
not be stated.

\subsection{The request channel, and an identity}
\label{sec:identity}

Requests come from two declared channels. The \emph{uninformed} channel draws a phrasing from a
shared list using only the seed: what someone says when they do not know what is wrong. The
\emph{informed} channel reveals the situation. The primary metric uses the uninformed channel.

\begin{proposition}
If the request text depends only on the seed, and each seed contributes one positive and one
negative pair, then every classifier trained on the text alone has AUC exactly $0.5$ on that
channel.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $T(s)$ be the text for seed $s$. The positives' texts and the negatives' texts are the same
multiset $\{T(s)\}$, so any score $g$ gives the two classes the same multiset of scores
$\{g(T(s))\}$. AUC is $\Pr[g(X)>g(Y)]+\tfrac12\Pr[g(X)=g(Y)]$ for $X$ a positive and $Y$ a
negative drawn uniformly. With identical multisets, $\Pr[g(X)>g(Y)]=\Pr[g(Y)>g(X)]$, so the AUC
is $\tfrac12$.
\end{proof}

So the uninformed channel's AUC of 0.500 on every intent certifies nothing about the phrasings.
The test that asserts it is a tripwire: it fails if the sampler starts to read the state. The
informed channel's AUCs, 0.962, 0.945, 1.000, 1.000 and 1.000 across the five intents, are the
real measurement, and show how much state-aware wording would leak. One consequence matters for
Section~\ref{sec:comparison}: on the uninformed channel, \emph{no} text scorer can tell sibling
resolutions apart, whatever its quality.

\paragraph{Where text similarity goes wrong.}
Table~\ref{tab:perfault} splits the errors by fault and by whether the state needed anything.
Of lexical similarity's 440 wrong fires, 318 are on fault-free states, where every fire is wrong
because nothing needed doing; for the cross-encoder, 283 of 409. So most of text dispatch's
error is \emph{firing at all}, not choosing the wrong sibling. On faulted states, lexical
similarity chose a wrong resolution on 122 of its 241 fires (51\%) and the cross-encoder on 126
of 279 (45\%), against 67\% for a uniform guess among three resolutions: better than chance, as
their floors say, and far from reliable. Precondition dispatch fired on no fault-free pair, and on
every faulted pair except 37 lock-file states where no admitted program's checks held.

\paragraph{What the comparison establishes.}
Each pair's acceptable set is a rule over the observed state, and the rule is ours. The state
reader fired on 190 of the 400 pairs, none wrongly, and on the held-out block on 200, none
wrongly, where precondition dispatch had 13, 8 and 21 mis-fires. So what these tables establish
is that a dispatcher that reads the state beats one that reads the request, and that compiled
preconditions are one such dispatcher. Whether executing checks adds anything over reading the
state is the question Sections~\ref{sec:real} and~\ref{sec:power} take up.

\paragraph{A reader of precondition dispatch's own evidence.}
The state reader sees fingerprint fields that no compiled program probes. So, after the fact, we
gave a decision tree per intent only what precondition dispatch reads: whether each of the
library's distinct preconditions holds on the state. That is 60, 60 and 57 features. The tree may
fire only a resolution its library holds a program for.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Precondition dispatch, recomputed from the same verdicts, reproduces Table~\ref{tab:synthetic}
  and the held-out counts exactly.
  \item The tree's wrong fires are 0, 0 and 0 on our states and 8, 8 and 5 on held-out states,
  against precondition dispatch's 13, 8 and 21 (Table~\ref{tab:settings}).
  \item Its fires are 187, 200 and 171 on our states, against 187, 187 and 189.
\end{itemize}
Reading the same facts with a learned tree is therefore at least as accurate as executing them as
a conjunction. On states we built, precondition dispatch's advantage over reading the state lies
neither in the facts it reads nor in how it combines them.

\paragraph{Held-out states, and a mechanism.}
On the held-out block four sources carried: one mis-fire in 480 fires. The lock-file source, which
moves the conflicted file to a nested path, a path with a space, a non-ASCII name or a dotfile,
did not: 41 of 101 fires were wrong, every one on a path with a space or a non-ASCII character.
The cause is a class of probe. Git quotes such paths in its default output, so a probe that
reads a path list without \code{-z} and compares, greps or splits it finds nothing. Each such
probe was an \emph{absence} check, so finding nothing meant it held, and the program fired. Two
of the mis-firing programs were \code{diverged}'s \code{discard}, which on a lock-file state
throws the local dependency change away. Admission passed them because no admit-seed path needed
quoting. Reading the libraries found at least 16 such probes in 10 of their 40 admitted programs.
The failure is general: an absence check fails \emph{open} when its reading of the state breaks.

\section{A pre-registered test on real lock-file merges}
\label{sec:real}

\subsection{Registration}

Before any case was mined we committed a registration whose SHA-256 is pinned in the code; the
measurement refuses to run unless the document is present, committed and unchanged. It fixes the
hypotheses, the ordered repository list, the case and label definitions, the analysis and the
decision rules. A pilot of 44 merges motivated one amendment, committed before the confirmatory
data was mined, and tests nothing.

\paragraph{Hypotheses.}
Rule 2: precondition dispatch rarely mis-fires, meaning the Wilson lower bound of its mismatch is
at most 0.10. H1: at matched coverage it mis-fires less than lexical similarity. H2: it
mis-fires less than the state reader. H3, added by the amendment: it fires less often than the
state reader on conflicts no resolution can express. Only H2 could license a sentence saying
that executing checks, rather than reading the state, is what wins.

\subsection{Cases and labels}

A \emph{conflicted case} is a two-parent merge whose sides both changed a lock file and whose
\code{git merge-tree} conflicts in it. A \emph{clean case} changed a lock file on both sides and
merged cleanly; per repository, as many clean cases as conflicted, the most recent first.
Criss-cross merges and submodule or binary conflicts are excluded and counted. The state is a
checkout of the first parent, tracking upstream's default branch at the second parent.

For each conflicted lock file the base's, parents' and merge's versions are read as multisets of
lines, $B$, $L$, $U$ and $M$. \code{take\_upstream} produces $U+(L-B)$ and \code{keep\_local}
produces $L+(U-B)$. A resolution is acceptable if what it produces equals $M$ for every conflicted
lock file, and only lock files conflicted. \code{merge} is acceptable on a clean case whose
committed tree equals the tree \code{git merge-tree} writes. A conflicted case with an empty
acceptable set is \emph{inexpressible}.

Sixteen of the 18 listed repositories were mined, in order, until the target of 247 cases from at
least 8 repositories was met; five yielded no case. The set has 274 cases from 11 repositories,
161 conflicted and 113 clean, and three exclusions. No repository exceeds the registered cap.

\section{A pre-registered external oracle for lock-file conflicts}
\label{sec:oracle}

Section~\ref{sec:real} showed that the lock-file intent's two line unions express almost none of
what maintainers commit. That is a fault of the formulation: a lock file is a set of resolved
packages, not a sequence of lines. A third study, registered before its data and on repositories
this project had never mined, asks whether the package-level formulation gives the task an
external oracle.

\subsection{Study 3: an external oracle}

\paragraph{Labels.}
Each conflicted lock file is read into its set of resolved $(\text{package}, \text{version})$
pairs, for every lock file study 1 counts: npm's two, yarn, pnpm, Cargo, poetry, Pipfile, uv,
Gemfile and composer. Formatting, order, checksums and the file format's version are ignored.
From the base's, local side's and upstream's sets $B$, $L$, $U$, four actions are computed with
no tool and no network:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{take\_local}: $L$;
  \item \code{take\_upstream}: $U$;
  \item \code{three\_way}: $(B\cap L\cap U)\cup(L-B)\cup(U-B)$;
  \item \code{three\_way\_unified}: the same, keeping within each package's compatible range only
  the highest version.
\end{itemize}
An action \emph{reproduces} a case when it gives exactly the maintainer's set on every conflicted
lock file. A case is \emph{covered} when some action reproduces it.

\paragraph{Hypotheses and analysis.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1:} mean coverage over repositories is above 0.10.
  \item \textbf{H2:} the unified three-way merge is closer than each side taken whole, by mean
  per-case Jaccard similarity to the maintainer's set.
\end{itemize}
A repository counts once. Each figure is a mean of per-repository means, with a 95\% t-interval
over repositories. A simulation from an exploratory pilot (30 conflicts, 6 repositories, already
seen) sized the study at 15 repositories (H1's power 0.85), and showed the t-interval
supporting H1 falsely at most 1.6\% of the time when coverage is exactly at the bound.
Repositories were chosen with study 1's feasibility count, which reads no merge commit's tree:
of 53 named candidates, all 27 with at least five lock-file conflicts were registered.

\paragraph{Result.}
The study ran as registered on 360 conflicts from the 27 repositories, at most 15 each. Both
hypotheses are supported:
\begin{itemize}
  \item coverage is 0.686 [0.601, 0.770] (pooled over cases, 0.683);
  \item the merge is closer than the local side by $+0.069$ [0.023, 0.115], and closer than
  upstream by $+0.113$ [0.069, 0.156].
\end{itemize}
Study 1's line unions reproduce 0.072 of the same cases, so two in three real conflicts have a
label that no one here wrote. Table~\ref{tab:oracle} breaks the result down by lock file.
\code{three\_way} alone reproduces the most cases (0.389). Where it misses, it misses by little:
its median per-case Jaccard is 0.996.

\subsection{The anatomy of a conflict (exploratory)}
\label{sec:anatomy}

Studies 3 and 4 say how often a computed merge reproduces the maintainer, not why. After both
had run, we read every conflicted file of both studies, 412 and 290, at all four commits. Each
package name falls into one of four kinds, by what the two sides did to its versions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{untouched}: neither side changed them;
  \item \emph{one-sided}: exactly one side did;
  \item \emph{convergent}: both changed them to the same versions;
  \item \emph{divergent}: both changed them to different versions.
\end{itemize}
Only a divergent package lacks a single answer that the three-way merge computes: it is a
conflict at the level of packages. In npm's family a name collects the versions of all of a
package's nested copies.

\paragraph{Most line conflicts are not package conflicts.}
One-sided names outnumber divergent ones 18 to 1 in study 3 (40,179 against 2,240) and 28 to 1 in
study 4 (37,588 against 1,330). In 49\% and 56\% of conflicted files no package diverges at all.
So half of the conflicts git reports in a lock file are not disagreements between the sides'
choices of package. They are the line format colliding: two sides that add or bump different
packages touch neighbouring lines. This is why study 1's line unions, which keep the format,
reproduce 0.07 of conflicts, while a merge of package sets reproduces most of them.

\paragraph{Divergence decides when the merge works.}
Table~\ref{tab:anatomy} shows how the predicted merge fares as divergence grows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No divergent package:} it reproduces 0.66--0.82 of files, in either family.
  \item \textbf{npm's family:} one divergent package almost always defeats it (0.00--0.12). The

  \item \textbf{One-version family:} it degrades gradually, from 0.42--0.65 with one divergent
  package to 0.14--0.15 with five or more. Unifying keeps the higher version, which is often the
  one kept.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Where packages diverge, maintainers keep their own side.}
Of the 340 files with a divergent package, the maintainer kept the first parent's versions on
every divergent package in 153 files, and the second parent's in 68; the rest mix. The first
parent is the branch the maintainer was on when they merged. In 176 of the 340 the merge matches
the maintainer on every other package, so the divergent packages alone stand between the merge
and the label. Settling each divergent package in favour of the local side reproduces 108 of
them, against 42 for the upstream side and 88 for the highest version.

\paragraph{Where nothing diverges, the manifest decides.}
The merge still misses 65 and 37 files in which no package diverges. Most often (52 and 36) the
maintainer did not take a change one side made; less often (23 and 4) they ran a resolver. A
maintainer who resolves \code{package.json} or \code{composer.json} by keeping one side's
constraint drops the other side's bump from the lock file, and nothing in the lock files shows
it. A merge restricted to what the merged manifest reaches recovers almost none of these, and it
breaks the composer files the plain merge reproduced. Maintainers' own \code{composer.lock}
files are fully reachable from their \code{composer.json} in only 7 of 233 cases, because merge
plugins and path repositories add requirements the manifest does not show.

\paragraph{Repositories differ.}
Coverage runs from 0.27 to 1.00 in both studies. In study 4, the share of a repository's cases
with a divergent package tracks that spread (Spearman $\rho=-0.65$, 20 repositories). In study 3
it does not ($\rho=-0.13$, 27), because low coverage with little divergence there mostly marks
maintainers who ran a resolver. \code{rust-lang/crates.io} is the clearest case: a divergent
package in 0.13 of its cases, coverage 0.27.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Always firing the merge is wrong on 0.56 and 0.45 of conflicts.} No fixed

  \item \textbf{``No package diverges'' is an executable precondition.} It is computed from the

  \item \textbf{It is still not rare.} Both intervals lie well above the 0.10 bound study 1
  registered for ``rarely mis-fires''. What remains is the manifest effect and resolver runs,
  which no check over the lock files can see.
\end{itemize}

So the structure of a conflict supplies a precondition worth having, and that precondition also
shows the limit of any dispatcher that reads only lock files. It was found after seeing both
studies, so it was a hypothesis. Study 5 (Section~\ref{sec:gate}) registered it and tested it on
fresh repositories.

\paragraph{What the oracle cannot label, and the ecosystem's tool.}
Of the conflicted files no computed action reproduces, 63 of 124 in study 3 and 12 of 67 in study
4 hold versions that none of the base, local or upstream files had. A maintainer ran a resolver
there, and no computed action can match that. For a dispatcher those are cases to refuse, or to
hand to the ecosystem's tool.

That tool can be replayed for npm, which can resolve against the registry as it stood on the
merge's date (\code{--before}). Started from either side's lock file, it reproduces the
maintainer's exact set on 14 of the 52 unreproduced \code{package-lock.json} files: 11 of 31
recombinations and 3 of 21 new resolutions. A first run failed outright on one repository's
files. The cause was npm refusing that project's required Node version (\code{engine-strict}),
which guards installation, not resolution. So a replay must pin the runtime as well as the tool.

Of the 172 conflicted \code{package-lock.json} files in both studies:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a computed action labels 120;
  \item the dated tool labels 14 more, for 0.78 in all;
  \item the remaining 38 are labelled by neither.
\end{itemize}
This was chosen after seeing the data, so it is not a test.

Two more ecosystems can be held to a date, and were piloted the same way on studies 3--5.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{pnpm} refuses versions younger than \code{minimumReleaseAge}, set to the minutes
  since the merge, and runs at the version each repository declares. It replays 18 of the 20
  unreproduced \code{pnpm-lock.yaml} files' own merges. From a side, it reproduces the
  maintainer on 5, all from one repository. Eight files depend on git tarballs from a host the
  sandbox's network policy blocks.
  \item \textbf{Composer}, through a plugin that drops versions younger than an age, follows
  Composer's documented procedure for a conflicted \code{composer.lock}. It takes one side's file
  and updates the root requirements the merged \code{composer.json} changed. It replays only 54
  of the 84 merges. Most failures pin a branch dependency, which the registry serves only at the
  branch's current head. From a side, it reproduces 9 of those 54. On 57 sides the side's file
  already satisfied the merged manifest, and the maintainer's file still differed: the
  maintainer changed more than the manifests required.
\end{itemize}
Bundler's dating plugin acts only on a full install and ignores the lock file, so it cannot be
used. Over the three ecosystems the dated tool recovers 30 of the 156 files the computed merge
missed, too few to label with, and no study was registered on it.

\paragraph{A second executed check: the tool's.}
The ecosystem's tool also offers a precondition that needs no date: does a side's lock file pass
the tool's own consistency check in the merge's tree (Section~\ref{sec:graders})? On the 610
\code{package-lock.json}, \code{pnpm-lock.yaml} and \code{composer.lock} files of studies 3--5,
a rule that fires the gate's merge, else the one side the tool accepts, else refuses, covers 0.69
of files with mismatch 0.32 [0.27, 0.36]. The gate alone covers 0.40 at 0.28, and each study
shows the same pattern. Against the obvious control the tool's own contribution is small:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the side it accepts is usually the first parent's, and maintainers mostly keep their own
  branch's versions;
  \item on the 176 divergent files where it fires, its side is right on 111, the first parent's
  on 91;
  \item paired, it wins on 20 files and never loses. That is structural: it takes the second side
  only when the first fails the check, and a failing side is rarely the maintainer's.
\end{itemize}
Twelve of those 20 files are one repository's. This is exploratory, on seen data, and still
wrong on a third of fires.

\paragraph{The five real gaps.}
All five survivors among the boolean swaps turn a failure into a pass, \code{ok=False} into
\code{True}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{config\_renamed}, three clauses: the renamed key set to a different value than the

\section{The registered follow-up study, and its power}
\label{sec:power}

\subsection{Design}

The exploratory finding is a hypothesis. We registered a second study before any of its data
exists. It treats builds as a population: 10 builds in each of two configurations, build $b$
permuting the order of the admit plan with seed $b$. Configuration~0 is the current gate.
Configuration~R also admits a program only if its preconditions refuse every state of a frozen set
$R$ of up to 40 real inexpressible conflicts from four admission repositories. Control-0 is the
library-scoped state reader fitted on the 56 states; Control-R is the same reader also fitted on
$R$, labelled ``fire nothing''. With $f(\cdot)$ the fire rate on the evaluation set's
inexpressible conflicts:

\begin{itemize}
  \item H1 (primary): $\overline{f(\text{Control-R}) - f(b)}_{b\in R} > 0$, the only comparison
  in which both sides have the same refusal evidence;
  \item H2: the same for configuration 0 against Control-0;
  \item H3: $\bar f_0 - \bar f_R > 0$;
  \item H4 (guard): configuration R's right-fire rate on clean cases is not lower than 0's by
  more than 0.10.
\end{itemize}

Each interval is a 95\% two-level bootstrap: each of 2000 resamples draws the evaluation
repositories with replacement, then each configuration's builds with replacement. H1--H3 are
supported if the lower bound is above 0, and H4 holds if it is above $-0.10$. Evaluation cases
come from 12 repositories no earlier stage touched, mined only after all 20 libraries are frozen,
until there are 100 inexpressible conflicts and 60 clean cases from at least 6 repositories. The
code implementing all of this is reviewed before any of $R$ is mined. Six choices the registration
leaves open, such as the per-repository cap and whether an $R$ refusal gets the one revision
other refusals get, were decided and recorded before any data.

\subsection{A Monte Carlo power analysis}

The registration states its power from a back-of-envelope interval half-width. We instead
simulate the study under explicit assumptions and apply its registered decision rules.

\paragraph{The generating model.}
Case $i$ in repository $r$ is fired on by build $b$ with probability
$\operatorname{logistic}(a_b+u_r+v_i)$, with $a_b\sim N(\operatorname{logit} m, s_b)$, $u_r\sim
N(0,s_r)$ and $v_i\sim N(0,s_c)$. A control is one dispatcher, so it is one draw. The build
spread $s_b=2.19$ is calibrated on study 1, whose three libraries fired on 22.5\%, 5.6\% and
81.9\% of the inexpressible conflicts; their logits have that standard deviation. The clean-case
build spread, calibrated the same way on study 1's right rates of 87, 112 and 112 of 113, is
2.03. The repository and case spreads have no anchor and are varied. The evaluation set has 100
inexpressible conflicts from 7 repositories (30, 25, 15, 10, 8, 7 and 5) and 60 clean cases in
the same proportions. Every reported rate is the realised mean, computed numerically, since a
centre on the logit scale is not a mean.

\paragraph{The bootstrap, validated.}
The registered bootstrap is pure Python and too slow for thousands of simulated studies, so the
analysis uses a vectorised copy. On three simulated studies it gives point estimates identical to
the registered code's and interval ends within 0.03, the difference being only which resamples
each draws. Each power figure is the share of 400 simulated studies, and each false-positive rate
the share of 2000, in which the hypothesis is supported.

\paragraph{What it shows.}
Table~\ref{tab:power} and Figure~\ref{fig:power} give the results.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The primary test's stated power holds.} The registration says H1 can detect a
  difference of about 0.3. At study 1's build spread, a true difference of 0.28 is detected with
  probability 0.80. Calmer builds lower that to about 0.25. H3's stated sensitivity of about 0.4
  is consistent too: 0.27 is detected with probability 0.60--0.70.
  \item \textbf{The false-positive rates are a little above nominal}: 2.8--3.8\% against 2.5\%.
  This is the percentile bootstrap's known slight anti-conservatism with only ten builds per
  group. It is small, and we report it rather than adjust the registered rule.
  \item \textbf{Results are insensitive to the unanchored spreads.} Setting the repository and
  case spreads to 0, or the repository spread to 1.0, or the case spread to 1.5, moves H1's power
  in the $s_b=2.19$, difference-0.28 scenario between 0.73 and 0.80.
  \item \textbf{The non-inferiority guard needs a different reading.} With no true cost at all, H4 holds
  in about half the simulated studies with a clean-case build spread of 1.0, and in only a fifth
  to a quarter with study 1's spread. The reason is arithmetic. Under the model, the builds' right
  rates on the 60 clean cases have a standard deviation $\sigma$ of about 0.10 at the calmer
  spread and 0.22 at study 1's. The difference of two means of ten builds then has standard error
  $\sqrt{2}\,\sigma/\sqrt{10}$, 0.045 or 0.097, so the 95\% interval's half-width from builds
  alone is 0.09 or 0.19, before resampling repositories widens it further. With a true difference
  of zero, the lower bound clears $-0.10$ only when the half-width happens to fall below the
  margin: about half the time at the calmer spread, and rarely at study 1's. More clean cases
  cannot fix that; only more builds or a calmer build process could.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The amendment it led to.}
As registered, rule 5 read ``H3 supported and H4 fails'' as ``refusal-tested admission buys
declining at the cost of clean merges''. Under the simulation, given H3 is supported and there is
no true cost, H4 fails most of the time, so that sentence would usually have been false. Because
the analysis preceded any data, the registration was amended (Amendment 1). H4 now has three
outcomes: it \emph{holds} when its interval's lower bound is above $-0.10$, it finds a
\emph{cost} when the upper bound is below $-0.10$, and otherwise non-inferiority is \emph{not
shown}. Rule 5 reports a cost only when H4 finds one. Nothing else in the registration changed.
Rerun under the amended rule, the simulation reports a cost in at most 0.5\% of studies with no
true cost (Table~\ref{tab:power}). The rule is now conservative in the other direction: a real
cost of 0.14--0.15 is reported in 4--12\% of studies, so at this size a cost will rarely be shown,
and a study that shows none has not ruled one out.
This is the use a power analysis is for: it changed what the study will be allowed to conclude
before it could see a result.

\paragraph{The executability gate \cite{wix2026gating}.}
In a deployed customer-care agent covering a ten-skill domain family, a recall-oriented semantic
matcher proposes candidate skills without looking at account state. A deterministic gate then
removes every candidate whose hard exit predicates hold on authoritative state, before its
description reaches the model. The guarantee is one-sided: passing the gate means no represented
exit condition holds, not that the user wants the skill. In a counterfactual replay of 1,000
conversations without the gate, the model selected a skill production would have blocked in 78,
7.8\%; matching plus gating cut skill-description context by 90.5\% against exposing all ten
descriptions. The authors claim no downstream tool-execution or customer-outcome effect.

Three points of contact follow.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The cascade.} Their architecture is recall then gate. Our hybrid arm is exactly
  that shape: retrieve the top $k$ by text, then fire the first whose preconditions hold. On our
  libraries the hybrid never mis-fired and never fired more often than the gate alone, and by
  construction it cannot: its fires are a subset of arm 3's. Capping $k$ only dropped correct
  fires: at $k=1$, 56--76 of arm 3's 187--189. But as the \emph{order} in which to try programs,
  stopping at the first that holds, text cut the checks evaluated by 29--37\% with identical
  decisions, against 20\% for trying the most specific program first
  (Table~\ref{tab:probecost}). So on our states retrieval before a gate is an efficiency device,
  not an accuracy one. That is consistent with their reported gains being in context, not in
  correctness.
  \item \textbf{One-sided checks fail open.} Their exit predicates block. Our held-out failure
  shows how blocking checks break: an absence check whose reading of the state silently fails
  reports ``absent'' and lets the program through. Hand-written predicates over a typed account
  API are less exposed than LLM-written shell probes over git's quoted output, but the failure
  mode is the same, and it is invisible to a funnel metric.
  \item \textbf{What neither measures.} Their counterfactual measures the model's selection
  error when the gate is removed, and ours measures the dispatcher's own error at matched
  coverage. Neither alone says how often a gated system runs the wrong skill on states nobody
  anticipated. Our real-merge study gives that figure for one domain: 204 wrong of 516 fires
  across three libraries.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The retrieval-risk benchmark \cite{ding2026rightfamily}.}
Each unit pairs a helpful skill with a query-specific risky sibling from the same capability
family, differing on an execution-controlling contract: a required resource, a precondition, a
procedure or an artifact. The metric is the harmful-sibling rate among the top $K$ retrieved,
HSR@$K$. The paper has changed title and size across versions; in its second version, public
retrievers had HSR@3 of 0.346--0.372 and a resolver that uses the contracts 0.007.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The same object, seen from two sides.} Their risky sibling is our same-intent
  negative class: a program for \code{take\_upstream} on a state where \code{keep\_local} is
  right. They measure whether retrieval \emph{exposes} it; we measure whether a dispatcher
  \emph{fires} it, at matched coverage, and require admission to refuse it.
  \item \textbf{Why our text scorers do so much worse.} Our text arms are wrong on 71--90\% of
  fires, far above their retrievers' HSR@3. The difference is not scorer quality. Their queries
  state the contract, so a contract-aware resolver can nearly eliminate exposure from text.
  Ours, on the uninformed channel, carry no information about the state by construction
  (Proposition~1), so no text scorer can separate siblings. The two results bracket text-based
  dispatch: it can work when the request states what distinguishes the right skill, and cannot
  when only the environment does.
  \item \textbf{What both leave out.} Both assume a right skill exists among the candidates. On
  real lock-file conflicts it almost never did, 1 of 161. There the right action is to refuse,
  and the measure is how often a dispatcher declines. A one-sided gate refuses only what its
  predicates anticipated; study 2 asks whether admission can select for refusal of what was not.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What executing a check buys.}
Four comparisons bear on this question, each holding the evidence fixed in a different way.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{On our states, nothing.} A tree reading the state's fingerprint matched
  precondition dispatch. A tree reading only the library's own precondition verdicts was at least
  as accurate as their conjunction (Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}). So the advantage lies neither
  in the facts the probes read nor in how they are combined.
  \item \textbf{On held-out states, it cost something.} Absence probes that silently failed to
  read git's quoted paths let wrong programs fire. That failure belongs to execution: a
  classifier over a fixed fingerprint has no probe to break.
  \item \textbf{On real merges, refusal.} The one place executing a check differed from reading
  the state was the compiled \code{merge} programs. They try the merge and refuse when it
  conflicts, a fact the reader's 56 training states rarely showed it (Section~\ref{sec:real}).
  \item \textbf{On real conflicts read as packages, a computed fact.} The best rule found is
  itself a precondition: fire only if no package diverges (Section~\ref{sec:dispatcher}). A
  registered study confirmed on fresh repositories that it helps and is not enough
  (Section~\ref{sec:gate}).
\end{enumerate}
Read together, the value of executing a check is not execution. A check can compute, on the live
state, a fact that no fixed set of features anticipated: a dry-run merge, a package-level diff.
It fails when that computation breaks silently.

\paragraph{Where wrong fires come from.}
Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} collects the sources of wrong fires this report found. They fall into
two groups.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The first three are failures of the dispatcher.} Reading the state, and checks
  that fail closed, remove them on states like ours.
  \item \textbf{The last three are failures of the formulation, and they dominate on real data.}
  The right answer was missing from the action space, or depended on something the dispatcher
  could not see.
\end{itemize}
Studies 3 and 4 moved the first of those three, by changing the unit from lines to packages.
That change raised the share of conflicts with a reproducing action from 0.07 to 0.69, more
than any choice of dispatcher did on these data.

\paragraph{Implications for compiled skills.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A check must fail closed.} An absence check has to distinguish ``found nothing''
  from ``could not read''. Admission should include states built to break the reading: odd
  paths, encodings, empty outputs.
  \item \textbf{The action space should use the domain's own unit.} The formulation decided more
  here than the dispatcher.
  \item \textbf{Refusal is an outcome to measure.} It has its own rate on cases where every fire
  is wrong, and it is not merely the absence of a fire.
  \item \textbf{An error rate needs someone else's labels.} The headline claim on our own
  labels, that precondition dispatch rarely mis-fires, failed on labels this project did not
  write.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Analytic flexibility.} The registered decisions are these: study 1's rule 2 and
  H1--H3; study 3's H1 and H2; study 4's H1--H4; study 5's H1--H3; and study 2's design and
  power. Everything
  marked exploratory was chosen after seeing the data it is reported on. That covers the
  library-scoped and probe-verdict readers, unification, the anatomy, the dispatch rules, the
  closure pilot and the npm replay. Each is reported as a hypothesis. Studies 4 and 5 have since
  turned two of them into tests.
  \item \textbf{Construct.} A label is the maintainer's commit, one acceptable resolution among
  possibly several. A fire that differs but would build and pass counts as wrong, so mismatch
  bounds harm from above. A blind labelling kit is built for measuring how often people accept a
  different answer: salted card identifiers, a key kept apart from the cards, and Cohen's
  $\kappa$ between raters. It awaits raters.
  \item \textbf{External.} The task family is git maintenance. Real repositories were chosen
  because they had at least five lock-file conflicts, and they are open-source projects, mostly
  PHP, JavaScript, Ruby, Rust and Python. The libraries come from one model at one temperature.
  \item \textbf{Statistical.} Cases cluster in repositories. Studies 3 and 4 average over
  repositories and vote by repository, and study 1 clusters its bootstrap. Study 1 has only three
  libraries, which is why study 2 treats builds as a population.
  \item \textbf{Mining.} Every study mines with \code{git merge-tree} in a cache where no
  repository's \code{.gitattributes} applies. A maintainer's working tree applies them, and a lock
  file declared binary conflicts whenever both sides change it. An audit read the \code{merge}
  attribute at each case's first parent, for all 1,123 cases of studies 1, 3, 4 and 5. It found
  the attribute set on three cases, two in study 1 and one in study 5, and none changes a registered
  decision.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope}

The results hold within the following scope, and each item names the work that extends it.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Labels.} On real merges a fire is right when it reproduces the maintainer's
  commit. The ecosystem's own tool, replayed at the merge's date, has been piloted for npm, pnpm
  and Composer (Section~\ref{sec:dispatcher}). Its consistency check agrees with the labels
  (Section~\ref{sec:graders}). Running each project's tests as a further oracle would admit
  resolutions that differ from the maintainer's yet build and pass; it was not run, because it
  executes the projects' code.
  \item \textbf{Builds.} The libraries come from one LLM at temperature 0. Their spread across
  three builds (Table~\ref{tab:repositories}) is why study 2 measures a population of twenty.
  \item \textbf{Domain and level.} The task family is git maintenance. The real-data tests cover
  lock-file merges from 11 repositories (study 1) and lock-file conflicts from 67 more
  (studies 3--5). This report measures dispatch decisions; the
  episode-level stages, cost per task and online learning, are implemented and not yet run at
  scale.
  \item \textbf{Text scorers.} The comparison covers three local scorers; an LLM judge that reads
  the state is implemented and registered as the next control.
  \item \textbf{Reproduction.} Every figure and table is regenerated from released files by
  committed scripts, and study 1's figures and labels were recomputed by scripts that import
  nothing from the project, so each analysis can be repeated independently.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On states we built and labelled, executable-precondition dispatch never mis-fired, but neither
did a classifier that only reads the state, and the request channel was built to carry nothing a
text scorer could use. On real lock-file merges, graded by what maintainers committed, the claim
that precondition dispatch rarely mis-fires was falsified. What survived is narrow: it fires
\code{merge} on clean merges where text similarity does not, and some libraries refuse conflicts
nothing can resolve. The follow-up study that tests refusal is registered; simulating it through
its own decision rules confirmed its primary test and led to an amendment of its guard, before
any of its data exists. A third registered study found what the task's labels should have been:
read as package sets, two in three real lock-file conflicts have an answer computed from the
merge's three sides that matches the maintainer's exactly, and a fourth found which merge to
compute for each ecosystem. Why it works is structural: half of those conflicts are the line
format colliding, not the sides disagreeing about a package. Where they do disagree, a
precondition that detects it halves the merge's error, but cannot reach ``rarely wrong'',
because the rest of what maintainers decide lives outside the lock files. A fifth registered
study confirmed both halves of that sentence on repositories nobody here had seen. The broader lesson is about evaluation: an error rate measured
against one's own labels says little until the same dispatcher faces states and labels someone
else made, and a registered design deserves the same scrutiny before its data as its results
receive after.
